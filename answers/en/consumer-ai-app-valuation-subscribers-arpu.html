How should investors value consumer AI apps: is subscribers times ARPU the right way?

Asked by: Investor · Judged 2026-10-11 · 张倩 Cynthia Zhang · FutureX Capital

Not directly: the diary's 2026-10-11 judgment is to value consumer AI revenue as concentrated-customer revenue, not as a subscriber base. In a16z's 10/5 ranking by US personal-card spend, the top 1% of payers contribute 19.5% of spend, more than the bottom half, at $903 a month per head (August, a total across all AI products, not one app's ticket size), while the median payer's $25 has barely moved. To value consumer AI, first ask whether that 1% is still there a year later and whose account the money comes from.

Judgment

Consensus (a16z, 10/5) reads the top payers as consumer AI's "first real market", an asset to scale up, and expects the paying base to broaden with new business models such as ads and transaction fees. The diary treats the top 1% as concentrated-customer risk: by its own arithmetic on the same panel, the average payer spends about $46.3 a month, and $903 is about 36 times the $25 median, so subscribers times blended ARPU cannot show how much revenue hangs on that 1%; until same-cohort retention and the paying entity can be shown, no subscription multiple. Second-order inference: in 2027, lead investors in late-stage consumer AI rounds and IPO underwriters will ask for the same-cohort retention and paying entity of top users; companies whose top revenue already runs through company billing, with provable retention, get priced on bottom-up SaaS net revenue retention, while those whose top revenue stays on personal cards, with retention undisclosed, get priced at a discount as usage revenue.

Facts

a16z's 7th-edition ranking, published 10/5, ranked apps by US personal-card spend for the first time (YipitData panel): the top 1% of payers contribute 19.5% of spend, more than the bottom 50% combined (16.6%); the top 1% spend $903 a month per head (as of August 2026), a total across all AI products on personal cards rather than one app's ticket size, up about 80% in 18 months; the median payer's $25 has barely moved; the share of eligible US consumers with a personal paid subscription to any of ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude rose from 2.1% to 4.5% in a year. Limits: the panel covers US personal cards only and cannot see company cards, and a16z states it cannot be read as a company's total revenue; the "top 1%" is a spending tier, and the source does not say whether it is the same people, so +80% cannot be read as same-cohort retention (inference). Over the same period, the share of Ramp's business customers making AI payments rose from 38.7% to 56.1%, a different population from the personal-card panel, read only as a parallel trend.

What to do

For investors: in diligence on new deals, do not multiply subscribers by blended ARPU; first break out the top 1%'s share of revenue, the same cohort's retention a year later, and the share already paid through company accounts; where a company cannot produce these, discount its top revenue as usage revenue and give no subscription multiple. For founders: move staff from user acquisition to invoicing and team billing, and design usage tiers around heavy top users, not the median. The diary notes that four current FutureX holdings are on a16z's 7th-edition list and that a16z and FutureX are both shareholders in Mistral (see the disclosure); this lens is for diligence on new deals only, not a valuation opinion on any holding.
