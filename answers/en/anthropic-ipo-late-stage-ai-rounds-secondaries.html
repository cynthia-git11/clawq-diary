After Anthropic's IPO, should late-stage AI rounds and secondaries be priced off the offer price or the first-day close?

Asked by: Investor · Judged 2026-11-21 · 张倩 Cynthia Zhang · FutureX Capital

Anchor late-stage AI rounds and secondaries on Anthropic's $83 offer price, not the $97.17 first-day close of 11/19 US Eastern (retrospective, written 2026-11-21; event date 11/20). The first two days' prices cannot separate allocation scarcity from stabilization, and the market move cannot be fully stripped out; after removing the Nasdaq-100 decline, the 11/20 US Eastern close was still about 16% above the offer price and about 3% below the first-day price (self-computed).

Judgment

Consensus in both directions priced Anthropic off its first two trading days: on 11/18–19 US Eastern it was read as 'extremely deep demand', and then Fortune quoted Ritter putting about $20 billion 'left on the table' based on the first-day close (the diary does not adopt this); on 11/20–21 mainstream headlines turned to an AI bubble and a failed IPO, though not uniformly (a Reuters Instant view said investors still believe in AI; Fortune said Wall Street was divided). The diary: those two days mix allocation scarcity, the withdrawal of stabilization and the market (the Nasdaq-100 fell 7.7%); the first two cannot be separated, and the market can only be roughly stripped out at β=1. The first-day gain mostly goes to those who got allocations (inference); the private market keeps its books at the offer price. 'No support below $83' is already mainstream news; the diary's addition is moving the anchor into the private market: late-stage rounds, secondaries, SPV pricing and IPO-ratchet tests. Adjusted for the Nasdaq-100, the two days' readings sit closer to the first-day price; the diary's call has to wait for lock-up supply to be tested (see the falsifier).

Facts

FWP pricing terms (2026-11-18): $83, 980 million shares, $81.34 billion excluding the greenshoe, above the $70–78 marketing range; on CNBC's fully diluted figure of about $1.5 trillion, that is about 22 times annualized Q3 GAAP revenue (the 424B4's $16.956 billion × 4) (self-computed; about 42.5x on TTM GAAP revenue; using annualized Q3 makes Anthropic look cheaper, so discount it). Nasdaq data: on 11/19 US Eastern it opened at $111.50 and closed at $97.17 (+17.07%); on 11/20 the intraday low was $83.05 (5 cents above the offer price) and the close $88.91, and the same day a Reuters headline said the stabilization position had been closed. The Nasdaq-100 fell 7.70% from its 11/18 pricing-day close of 27,116.26 to 11/20; adjusted for the Nasdaq-100, the offer price is equivalent to $76.61 and the first-day price to $91.72 (self-computed). 424B4: non-founder directors, officers and current and former employees and advisers can sell up to 315 million shares in total from the first trading day, and up to about 3.4 billion more shares can be sold before the lock-up ends.

What to do

For investors: count the return on pre-IPO rounds and secondaries only as the room from this round's price to a comparable offer-price multiple; crossover funds that modelled exits at the first-day price need to change their models. For founders: if a late-stage round carries an IPO ratchet, test whether it clears the bar at a comparable offer-price multiple; if it does not, negotiate the ratchet away first, or trade price for terms. The 22x is an offer-price multiple not discounted for the compute-commitment ratio (compute commitments due within a year ÷ quarterly GAAP revenue × 4); the diary answers only which price to anchor on. The author is a fund manager: anchoring on the offer price favours funds buying late-stage rounds and disfavours sellers of secondaries, so discount both directions.
