The new US rule requires licenses for overseas data centers serving Chinese-headquartered tenants: how should investors discount that rental revenue and the facilities' valuation?

Asked by: Investor · Judged 2026-11-21 · 张倩 Cynthia Zhang · FutureX Capital

The diary's call (retrospective, written 2026-11-21, covering the news around 11/14): carve out the revenue from China-headquartered tenants and discount it by the odds of a license, with the facility's equity taking one more layer of discount than its debt (an inference); if the 11/12 rush to rent lifted utilization, keep that out of the valuation. The basis is the BIS interim final rule, in effect since 11/19: an overseas data center providing more than 12,000,000 aggregate TPP (about 758 H100s, author's calculation) of compute to a company headquartered in China (D:5) or Macau needs a case-by-case license, and existing facilities must get one by 2027-02-17 (author's calculation).

Judgment

The consensus by the writing date (11/18–20) was that loans are under pressure and that overseas clouds and GPU lessors serving mainly Chinese customers are hit most directly. The diary departs on one point only: lenders protect themselves first with early-repayment clauses, so more of the discount lands on the facility's equity, which is why equity takes one more layer of discount than debt (an inference); high termination fees and larger prepayments push part of it back onto tenants (an inference). Licenses are reviewed case by case (§744.24(c)), with no presumption of denial, so the discount is a probability discount (an inference); but for an existing facility that is not licensed, the rule says it faces further export restrictions, which read literally would fall on the whole facility's future chip purchases, and expansion for non-Chinese tenants in the same facility would also depend on license status, so carving out only the China-tenant revenue is a floor (an inference).

Facts

Unemployment Labs said on 11/12 that six Chinese open models carry Blackwell traces, while the report states it cannot tell where the chips were; on the report's best estimates for five training runs, each used 3,500–11,500 H100 equivalents, 4.6–15 times the threshold (author's calculation; the report itself says it is unsure of its scale estimates). The rule, 91 FR 74970, was released on 11/16 and published and effective on 11/19: the applicants are the exporter and the facility's owner or operator, not the Chinese tenant; the exporter must be able to obtain customer due-diligence information from the overseas facility; the rule itself estimates about 100 new license applications a year. §744.24 does not distinguish by the facility's host country; exporting advanced chips to, or transferring them within, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Brazil needs a separate license, with US-headquartered recipients or users exempt after reporting under §743.10. Lenders are asking for high termination fees, larger prepayments and early-repayment clauses (Bloomberg via TrendForce 11/20, secondhand); Moonshot AI cancelled a $150 million chip deal in Indonesia on 11/19 (The Information, headline only), counted only as an after-the-event reading. Reuters reported on 11/12 that Chinese AI companies were rushing to rent overseas Nvidia capacity, and Techmeme's summary that day gave fear of tighter export-control enforcement as the cause; no source reported that the rush had already lifted utilization, so the text says only "if it lifted". As of 11/21 no report was found of BIS granting a §744.24 license to any facility serving Chinese tenants. The tenants named in the falsifier are Moonshot AI, Alibaba and Tencent.

What to do

For investors: in the facility model, carve out China-headquartered tenant revenue and discount it by license odds, and if the 11/12 rush to rent lifted utilization, keep that out of the valuation; the early-repayment clauses lenders want push the risk onto facility shareholders (an inference), so equity takes one more layer of discount than debt (an inference). Put new money into US domestic capacity, or into non-China-tenant capacity outside the four Southeast Asian countries and Brazil. Watch the 2027-02-17 license deadline for existing facilities, which will force license status into disclosure. For compute-provider founders: write the termination fees and prepayments lenders want into your leases, and swap the tenant if the license fails. This is a general due-diligence approach; it does not apply to any FutureX holding and does not signal any action on one.
